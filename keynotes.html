SWEDEN

Görel Fred

KEYNOTE

The Family, Therapy and Hope

Why do we continue? With all the heartbreaking stories we hear, all the children who suffer, all the parents at their wits’ end. With all the powerlessness that surrounds us, why do we dedicate our lives to something so difficult?

I seriously considered leaving this field. When the sense of powerlessness engulfed every part of me, I was ready to give up my dream of becoming a family therapist.

But I didn’t.

Instead, I learned—especially from the people I met in therapy—that powerlessness can give way to hope. I learned how joy and creativity can emerge through meeting and connecting with others.

In this presentation, I will share what I have found helpful in dealing with powerlessness during my nearly 40 years in the field of family therapy.

For me, it has been about understanding that we cannot take responsibility without having power. That we can fight shame, but not necessarily guilt. And, perhaps most importantly, truly accepting that it is hard because it is hard.

There are no easy solutions. Good advice is rarely of any help. We do what we do because, in that particular moment, our way of coping seems to give us more than it takes—even when the alternatives are no better.

If I want to be of any help to others, I need to keep practicing listening to the reasoning behind their choices.

If we want to connect with others, we have to risk being vulnerable ourselves. And how exhausting that can be!

Would it really be too much to ask that, after 40 years in the field, I should finally have arrived at a position of knowing?

Vulnerability once pushed me deep into powerlessness—but it didn’t end there.

I stayed. I learned. I rejoiced. I squirmed. And I came to understand that if vulnerability doesn’t break you, it might just become one of your greatest gifts.

This is a presentation about the power, joy and hope that exist in spite of what we encounter.

Or perhaps they exist because of what we encounter?

ABOUT THE SPEAKER

Görel Fred is a social worker, licensed psychotherapist, supervisor and author. She is the author of Reconciling with Your Childhood (and Maybe Your Parents) and hosts The Reconciliation Podcast. She has appeared on television and radio, as well as in several major newspapers and magazines, including SvD, Aftonbladet and Vi Föräldrar. In 2023, she was named Sweden’s Family Therapist of the Year.

DENMARK

Lene Tanggaard

KEYNOTE

Impure pedagogy

Lene Tanggaard will, in her keynote for us, speak about the concept of impure pedagogy, which is primarily presented in the books Impure Pedagogy 1, 2, and 3 (from 2011, 2014, and 2017). These works were a kind of professional protest against the instrumentalization of pedagogy. The books argue that pedagogy, in its essence, is a living historical practice that cannot be made “pure” through evidence-based approaches and “what works” thinking without destroying pedagogy itself. This protest has had a certain impact in various places. Now, the impure protest has reached pedagogy itself, where it is meant to act in a more constructive way. Impure pedagogy has become pedagogy as such — which, in a sense, it always was.

Pedagogy is the art of educating, shaping, or forming human beings, and this always takes place in the light of particular values. This means that teaching or other pedagogical interventions never work in a pure way. Impure pedagogy reminded us of this normativity and of the fact that so-called pure pedagogy — with its culturally neutral desire for control — was not even “pure pedagogy,” because it was not pedagogy at all. Now, impure pedagogy also wants to be what it has always been: pedagogical. And in doing so, it reconnects with the concept of bildung (formation), which pure pedagogy has otherwise sought to push out of our part of the world.

ABOUT THE SPEAKER

Lene Tanggaard is Professor at Aalborg University and an internationally recognized researcher, speaker, and author specializing in creativity, vocational education, learning, and qualitative research. She previously served as Principal of the Design School Kolding (2019–2025) and has extensive leadership experience in academia and research. She has published widely in international and national journals and books, supervised numerous PhD students, and led research and development projects across disciplines and complex organizational settings. She also serves on several boards and national committees and is a member of the Danish Council for Research and Innovation Policy.

FINLAND

Hanna Hakomäki

KEYNOTE

On a Shared Field – Children, Parents and Therapists as Active Collaborators in Family Therapy

Children are often present in family therapy, yet adults may struggle to see children as equal partners in adult-led processes. A child’s natural way of expressing experience is not necessarily verbal. Through art and interaction, children communicate emotions, intentions and experiences, create shared meanings, and shape relationships.

Listening to children therefore means more than hearing their words: it requires attending to their verbal, embodied, emotional and artistic ways of communicating. From a relational perspective, listening is reciprocal: children, parents and therapists respond to and transform one another’s meanings. In this shared field, listening becomes a foundation for participation and shared agency.

My journey began almost thirty years ago. While developing music therapy methods with children, I faced something unknown. Rather than imposing an adult-designed therapeutic framework, I followed children’s ideas and ways of communicating. Listening to them opened possibilities so powerfully they changed my work. Their musical inventions became a means of expressing and sharing difficult, unformulated, or unbearable experiences, emotions and memories.

What began as listening to children’s musical narratives gradually expanded into Creative Family Psychotherapy, embracing all art forms. Throughout, the focus has remained on a shared field where children, parents and therapists participate actively, regardless of developmental or functional differences. Art provides a relational and embodied medium for encountering one another beyond predetermined roles and creating more attuned parent-child interaction.

This keynote explores how children’s initiatives and ways of knowing, expressing and relating have guided therapeutic development, and invites us to consider open-minded possibilities for family therapy.

ABOUT THE SPEAKER

Hanna Hakomäki, PhD, Docent in Music Therapy (Children’s Music Therapy), graduated as a music therapist in 1990 and completed her PhD in 2013. She completed her studies in Family and Couple Psychotherapy at the University of Jyväskylä in 2017. She is also a Licensed Supervisor (Tampere University in 2011). For the past ten years, she has worked in Child Psychiatry at HUS Helsinki University Hospital with children and their families affected by severe mental health disorders and complex family dynamics.

Hakomäki continuously explores and develops creative psychotherapeutic approaches to meet the needs of children and families in specialised healthcare. She is the developer of the Storycomposing (Tarinasäveltäminen) music therapy method, from which both Family Music Psychotherapy and Creative Family Psychotherapy have evolved through her long-standing child-centred clinical and research work.

NORWAY

Knut Tore Sælør
& Rose-Marie Bank

KEYNOTE

Hope as a relational practice in times of despair

Hope is widely regarded as a fundamental dimension of human life. It becomes particularly important when we experience adversity, illness, or periods in which life feels difficult and overwhelming. At the same time, hope is a concept that can be difficult to define and grasp. It may appear abstract, elusive, or philosophical, and is often understood as an individual resource or personal characteristic. In this presentation, we argue for an alternative understanding of hope as a relational and contextual phenomenon, closely connected to everyday life, living conditions, and social relationships. Furthermore, we explore how hope can be understood as a practice rather than as a trait, emotion, or expectation. From this perspective, hope is something that can be co-created, sustained, and nurtured through relationships and meaningful action, including in situations marked by uncertainty, doubt, and despair. Experiences of change or recovery, rarely depend on becoming symptom-free or achieving a perfect life. Rather, they are closely connected to the possibility of living a meaningful life together with others.

The presentation draws on research, theory, our experiences as nurses, and lived experience gained through a life marked by both ups and downs. We highlight how seemingly small everyday actions, trusting relationships, and the concept of reasonable hope may contribute to recovery, growth, and change for the better. We argue that hope is not primarily something people have, but something people do together. This perspective has important implications for how professionals encounter and support people facing challenging life circumstances.

ABOUT THE SPEAKERS

Knut Tore Sælør

Knut Tore Sælør is a registered nurse with a background from the field of mental health. He holds a master’s degree in clinical health work and a PhD from the Faculty of Psychology at the University of Bergen, Norway. He is currently an associate professor at the University of South-Eastern Norway. Throughout his academic life, hope and recovery in its different contexts, shapes, and forms has been a common thread in his work.

Rose-Marie Bank

Rose-Marie Bank is a registered nurse holding a Master's degree in Clinical Mental Health Practice, as well as postgraduate training in collaborative mental health research. She is affiliated with the Centre for Mental Health and Substance Abuse at the University of South-Eastern Norway, where she contributes as a co-researcher and research assistant. Her research interests encompass mental health and horticulture, human–nature relationships in the context of mental health, recovery-oriented activities and environments, and service user involvement in research.

Drawing on her lived experience of mental health challenges, Rose-Marie integrates experiential knowledge into her teaching and supervision of students in the health and social care disciplines.
