SUBPLENARY

Emotionally Focused Family Therapy (EFFT): What Makes It Unique?

Marianne Gustafsson

Read abstract & biography

Abstract

Emotionally Focused Family Therapy (EFFT), developed by Dr. Sue Johnson together with colleagues including Gail Palmer and James Furrow, is an attachment-based, experiential model of family therapy that extends the principles of Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) to the family system. Building on decades of research supporting EFT with couples, EFFT provides a distinctive framework for understanding and transforming family distress through attachment, emotional bonding, and relational repair.

EFFT conceptualizes family distress not primarily as a problem of behaviour, parenting skills, or individual pathology, but as a disruption of attachment bonds and blocked emotional responsiveness within the family system. A central premise is that parents are the primary resource for children’s security and resilience. Under conditions of trauma, chronic stress, loss, or relational injury, caregivers may lose access to their natural caregiving system and their capacity for emotional availability and protection. At the same time, children and adolescents may struggle to express attachment needs in ways that can be received.

EFFT helps therapists transform these relational blocks by restoring parents’ access to their caregiving system while supporting children and adolescents in expressing underlying vulnerabilities and needs for connection. The model is distinctive in its experiential focus, using emotion as both the target and the agent of change. Through enactments, emotional processing, and corrective attachment experiences, families are supported in creating new patterns of safety, responsiveness, and connection.

For experienced family therapists, EFFT offers a powerful way of meeting the whole family system while keeping attachment and emotion at the centre of therapeutic change. This seminar will introduce the theoretical foundations, clinical process, and key interventions of EFFT, illustrated through clinical examples from a Swedish context.

Biography

Marianne Gustafsson is a social worker, licensed psychotherapist, systemic therapist, teacher, and supervisor in systemic psychotherapy. She is an internationally certified Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) Trainer, Supervisor, and Therapist through the International Centre for Excellence in Emotionally Focused Therapy (ICEEFT).

With more than 25 years of clinical experience in Swedish Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and private practice, Marianne has specialized in family therapy, attachment-based approaches, and emotionally focused interventions with couples, individuals, and families.

Since 2013, she has been deeply engaged in the development and dissemination of Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) in Sweden, initially focusing on couples therapy (EFCT), and later expanding her work to Emotionally Focused Individual Therapy (EFIT) and, with particular dedication, Emotionally Focused Family Therapy (EFFT).

Marianne is the founder and director of Livsverk and co-director of Stockholm EFT Center, where she provides psychotherapy, supervision, and training for mental health professionals. She combines systemic family therapy traditions with contemporary attachment science and experiential practice.

She is engaged in clinical writing and research, including publications in Fokus på Familjen, a forthcoming article on EFFT, and the ”Stronger Together” research project at Uppsala University, focusing on EFT-based interventions for parents of children diagnosed with cancer.

Marianne’s passion is supporting therapists and families in creating secure emotional bonds, resilience, and healing through meaningful therapeutic relationships.

SUBPLENARY

Social health: Bringing relationships into the heart of healthcare

Family and network perspectives as a foundation for health-promoting practice

Øyvind Reehorst Kalsås

Read abstract & biography

Abstract

Why do social relationships remain at the margins of healthcare when their importance for health is so well established?

Health is commonly understood and addressed through its physical and mental dimensions, while the social dimension has received considerably less attention. Yet people experience illness, recovery, crisis and major life transitions within families, relationships, social networks and communities.

This presentation explores social health as a distinct dimension of health, alongside physical and mental health, and considers what such a perspective might mean for professional practice. Particular attention is given to family and network-oriented approaches as ways of working directly with the relational and social conditions that shape health and well-being.

For family therapists and other professionals with a systemic and relational orientation, this raises an important question: What changes when relationships are understood not only as a context for health problems, but as part of health itself?

Drawing on the WHO Ottawa Charter’s conceptualization of health promotion, the presentation explores how healthcare can move beyond an individualised understanding of health towards practices that recognise and strengthen relationships, belonging, participation and social resources. It also considers the wider implications for healthcare services: How might services work with families and networks rather than treating them primarily as resources surrounding the individual patient?

Biography

Øyvind Reehorst Kalsås, PhD, LCSW, is a family therapist, researcher, and educator at Western Norway University of Applied Sciences (HVL), where he serves as Head of the Department of Welfare and Participation. His research explores social health, grief and bereavement, substance use and the role of families, social networks, and dialogical practices in psychosocial support and care.

He is the author of the book chapter “Social health” which is part of the new norwegian antology “Psykososialt arbeid ved kriser”.

SUBPLENARY

Studying Embodied and Multimodal Interaction in Couple Therapy Processes

Virpi-Liisa Kykyri & Anne Lukkarinen

Read abstract & biographies

Abstract

Interest in embodied and multimodal interaction in psychotherapy has grown rapidly in recent years. The Relational Mind in Events of Change in Therapeutic Dialogues project has investigated these phenomena for almost 14 years. The project is based on a unique multimodal dataset comprising couple therapy sessions with synchronized multi-camera video recordings, physiological measurements, process questionnaires, and video-assisted individual Stimulated Recall Interviews.

Research based on this dataset has produced novel evidence on interpersonal synchrony in couple therapy. The project was the first to demonstrate physiological and behavioral synchrony between participants and to show that synchrony is associated with therapeutic alliance and therapy progress. Studies have also identified links between speech, physiological synchrony, and movement synchrony. More recent work has moved beyond whole-session analyses to examine synchrony in specific interactional episodes, linking synchrony dynamics with emotional processes and interactional content through mixed-methods approaches.

In this sub-plenary session, Virpi-Liisa Kykyri will provide an overview of the Relational Mind research, with a particular focus on recent findings. Anne Lukkarinen will then present the design of her doctoral study on couple therapy involving clients with borderline personality disorder.

Lukkarinen’s study investigates changes in interpersonal physiological synchrony and therapeutic alliance during meaningful moments in couple therapy. Guided by the Flexible Multimodal Synchrony theory, the study employs a mixed-methods process research design combining physiological measurements, video analysis, and questionnaires. The dataset will include 30 couples and their therapists. Participants are recruited through adult psychiatric services within the Wellbeing Services County of North Savo following a referral to couple therapy.

Data will be analyzed using windowed correlation-based methods and qualitative content analysis. The study is expected to generate new knowledge about the role of interpersonal synchrony in couple therapy for borderline personality disorder. The findings may benefit clients, couples, families, and professionals and contribute to improved treatment outcomes and more effective mental health and social services.

Biographies

Virpi-Liisa Kykyri

Virpi-Liisa Kykyri, PhD, is Professor of Psychology and Vice Head of the Department of Psychology at the University of Jyväskylä, Finland. She leads the Multimodal Interaction Laboratory, part of the national MAGICS research infrastructure. Her research focuses on embodied processes in social interaction in clinical and workplace settings, using multimodal and mixed-methods approaches.

With the Embodied Eureka Research Collective, a multidisciplinary team of researchers that she leads, Kykyri investigates interpersonal attunement and synchrony, examining how people connect, co-regulate, and respond to one another through physiology, movement, emotion, and language. Kykyri has published extensively in international peer-reviewed journals and edited volumes.

Anne Lukkarinen

Anne Lukkarinen is a doctoral researcher in the Doctoral Programme in Psychology at the University of Jyväskylä, Faculty of Education and Psychology. Her research examines physiological attunement and the therapeutic alliance in couples therapy involving individuals with borderline personality disorder.

Her particular interests include nonverbal interaction, autonomic nervous system activity, and physiological synchrony between participants. She holds a Master's degree in Health Care and is a clinical expert in mental health and substance abuse work. She has worked as a registered nurse and as a dialectical behaviour therapy therapist.

In addition, she is completing training as a systemic family and couples psychotherapist at the University of Jyväskylä and is expected to graduate in May 2027.

SUBPLENARY

From Relational Patterns to Responses and Dignity – Response-Based Practice in Family Therapy

Anne Saxtorph

Read abstract & biography

Abstract

How can a Response-Based approach broaden our perspective and bring hope into family therapy?

This presentation explores how Response-Based Practice can sharpen our attention to language, power, dignity, and people’s responses to what they are subjected to. A central question is how we, as family therapists, can avoid making language of criticism, degradation, violence, and control and let it appear as mutual through our language when responsibility and power are not equally distributed.

Using concrete practice examples, I will demonstrate what a micro-level contextual analysis can involve: What is actually happening within social and relational contexts? Who is doing what to whom? How do individual family members respond? And how can Response-Based questions make power, oppression, resistance, and efforts to preserve dignity visible, rather than focusing solely on relational patterns and positions?

I will also briefly address how the understanding of power in Response-Based Practice differs from the perspectives of Bateson and Foucault.

Particular attention will be given to children and young people. A child experiencing anxiety, sadness, or acting-out behaviour can easily come to be seen as the bearer of the family’s pathology. But what becomes possible if, instead, we explore the child’s actions as responses and acts of resistance to concrete circumstances, rather than merely as symptoms? What is the child trying to protect, preserve, express, gain access to, or respond against? And what kind of dignity is the child striving for and longing for?

The underlying premise is that oppression takes place within social contexts, and that responses therefore cannot be understood in isolation within the individual or simply as dysfunction, even when the consequences of a response may be negative. Family therapy thus also becomes a practice of making power visible, clarifying responsibility, and highlighting the dignity of children and young people, including their claim to recognition and redress.

Biography

Anne Saxtorph is an authorized psychologist, educated at the University of Copenhagen in 1995, with more than 30 years of clinical experience in Denmark. She is a specialist in psychotherapy, family therapist, supervisor and partner in ‘Narrative Perspektiver’ with her collages, in Copenhagen.

Her work includes therapy, teaching at women’s shelters, institutions, and she arrange supervision in institutions and running a family therapy training programme. Her practice is grounded in systemic and narrative, norm-critical and Response-Based approaches, last part inspired by Allan Wade, Shelly Dean, Cathy Richardson and Linda Coates from Canada, with a particular focus on language, power, dignity and responses in social and relational contexts.

SUBPLENARY

What Do We Do Next? Navigating Complexity in Family Therapy

An Introduction to Integrative Systemic Therapy

Kristoffer J. Whittaker & Lennart Lorås

Read abstract & biographies

Abstract

Family therapy rarely follows a straight line. Family members may understand the problem differently, alliances can become fragile, and even well-intended interventions can fail to create meaningful change. How can therapists hold onto direction amid this complexity?

Drawing on our book, Integrative Systemic Therapy for Families with Children and Adolescents: A Clinician's Handbook, this subplenary introduces Integrative Systemic Therapy (IST), a practical framework that connects systemic theory, psychotherapy research, and clinical decision-making.

Through vivid clinical illustrations, we demonstrate how IST helps therapists develop shared understandings of problems, identify constraints that keep difficulties in place, and adapt therapy when progress stalls. We give particular attention to engaging children and adolescents, building strong alliances with all family members, and using feedback to determine what the family needs next.

Participants will leave with a fresh way of understanding therapeutic complexity, and practical ideas they can bring directly into their work with families.

Biographies

Kristoffer J. Whittaker

Kristoffer J. Whittaker, PhD, is a clinical psychologist and specialist in family psychology. He works at Nedre Romerike Family Counselling Office and is affiliated with the Research Institute at Modum Bad, Norway. His clinical and research interests include Integrative Systemic Therapy, mediation, therapeutic alliances, dyadic processes, and routine outcome monitoring. He combines clinical practice with research, writing, and doctoral supervision.

Lennart Lorås

Lennart Lorås, D.Sys.Psych, is Professor at Western Norway University of Applied Sciences and Professor II at VID Specialized University, Norway. His teaching and research encompass systemic family therapy, clinical practice, and qualitative research methods. He has authored and edited several books on family therapy, relational practice, and research methods.

SUBPLENARY

”Just Dance” - Creativity in Family psychotherapy practice

Tanja Pihlaja

Read abstract & biography

Abstract

This presentation is about creativity in family therapy practice. The theoretical background is based on a relational and holistic view of the human being and a dialogical and embodied way of doing family therapy.

The performance "TansSiksi" aims to bring to light the findings of a three-year family therapy process through dance. The presentation consists of two parts: a dance piece and a theoretical lecture. It is inspired by a three-year (2021–2024) family therapy process in which dance and embodiment played an integral role. As the work of concluding the process with the client began in the spring of 2024, we considered how to bring the process to a close and capture its meaningful moments—moments that can be viewed from the perspectives of both the client and the therapist. This sparked the idea of creating a dance piece that utilizes choreography. The Dance lasts 10–12 minutes - the choreography is developed in a client-centered manner, illustrating the core insights of the therapy.

Dialogical family therapy includes different kinds of listening processes – based on the question ”What has happened to you?” When you are listening as a therapist, you need to be open to different levels of experience. In this case, we were very interested about the sensomotoric level.

Lecture is following the guidelines from open dialog- particularly presence, a need-based attitude, attunement, responsiveness, and the ability to remain with uncertainty. Important theoretical point of view comes from the ideas of using expressive arts. This approach not only enables survivors to express experiences that defy verbalization, but also helps them to transform and integrate the trauma. When using creativity in a family therapy process, it is possible to see relationships from a new perspective, regain a sense of aliveness, and imagine a new future and new identity.

Biography

Tanja Pihlaja, M.Soc.Sc. is working as Clinical Supervisor, Dance Movement Therapist and Couple- and Family Psychotherapist and Dialogical trainer and EMDR-Therapist in her private practice Tuntumaa Oy. Open Dialog, embodied approach, authentic movement and systemic approach have included her way of doing clinical work with groups, couples and families over twenty years.

Her way of working is trauma sensitive. She has worked widely in social and healthcare services from psychiatry to child custody care. She is interested about different levels of experiences and creativity to integrate them with reflective work - which is happening both vertical and horizontal ways. She is a happy Chair of The Finnish Family- and Couple Therapy Association.

SUBPLENARY

Hope begins with a baby

Soffía Bæringsdóttir

Read abstract & biography

Abstract

The transition to parenthood is a major developmental change with in the family system. It is often a time of hope and excitement but also time of vulnerability. New parents are often very aware of the world around thmen and of the challenges their child may face in the future. Becoming a parent at uncertain times can bring fears and worries about what lies ahead for the family.

This presentation explores how we as therapists can bring hope with curiosity about the baby in a therapeutic conversation. Therapeutic curiosity can strengthen parental mentalisation and create hope when parents feel overwhelmed or stuck. We look at how family therapists can help parents with strength based approaches to help parents notice and integrate small and meaningful moments of connection.

Biography

Soffía Bæringsdóttir is a Family Therapist (MA) from the University in Iceland. She holds a Professional Certificate in Women and Substance Use from University College Dublin, Ireland, is a licensed Supervisor and newly VIG - accredited. Soffía has specialised in Parent–Infant bonding in Iceland. She works within Icelandic healthcare services, in Familyteam 0-5 years (Fjölskylduteymi 0-5 ára) supporting families with a particular focus on attachment and perinatal mental health.

For more than 15 years, she has provided education, supervision, and support for parents and professionals. She regularly teaches and presents on topics including infant bonding, grief and loss, attachment, and compassionate family-centered care. Soffía has presented internationally on several occasions.

Soffía is the Chairman of the Icelandic Family Therapy Association and has co-organised several international events. She lives in Reykjavík with her husband and three children.

SUBPLENARY

Coming off psychiatric drugs – a systemic perspective

Anders Sørensen

Read abstract & biography

Abstract

Psychiatric drugs are prescribed to individuals, yet coming off them often affects the people around them as well. Withdrawal can cause profound changes in emotions, behaviour, and everyday functioning; some temporary, some lasting. These changes are frequently difficult to understand, especially when they resemble the problems for which the drug was originally prescribed. How they are understood can have major consequences for the person withdrawing and for those close to them.

This lecture explores psychiatric drug withdrawal and tapering from a systemic perspective. Drawing on research and clinical experience, I will explain why stopping psychiatric drugs can be difficult, how withdrawal can be mistaken for relapse, and how to taper slowly and gradually.

Withdrawal also takes place within relationships. Family members may become frightened or uncertain when someone changes dramatically during a taper, and their understanding of what is happening will inevitably influence how they respond. Therapists can play an important role in helping families make sense of these experiences and in creating conditions that support a safer withdrawal process.

The lecture will offer practical knowledge about gradual, individualized tapering alongside a broader discussion of how withdrawal is understood within families and treatment systems. Bringing the drug itself into our systemic understanding can change how we make sense of difficulties that emerge during withdrawal and open up new possibilities for support, recovery, and hope.

Biography

Anders Sørensen, PhD, is a Danish clinical psychologist and researcher specializing in psychiatric drug withdrawal, tapering, and psychotherapy. His research has examined antidepressant withdrawal, receptor occupancy, tapering methods, and clinical guidelines.

He is the author of Crossing Zero: The Art and Science of Coming Off – and Staying Off – Psychiatric Drugs and teaches clinicians internationally about psychiatric drugs, withdrawal, and psychological approaches to severe emotional distress. His work focuses on helping people make sense of their experiences in ways that integrate biological, psychological, relational, and social perspectives.
